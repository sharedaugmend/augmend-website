The future of care delivery support is immersive.

Carefully crafted immersion holds attention in a way screens rarely do, and that's exactly why we cannot let it become dystopian.

## Let's be honest and start from the facts

There aren't enough staff, yet care needs people, especially nurses, for quality and compassion. Federal projections expect the United States to be short of registered nurses through 2038, the end of the projection period. The gap is about 8% nationally in 2028, and 24% outside metropolitan areas. For licensed practical nurses it is wider still: by 2038, supply is projected to meet only about 70% of demand.¹

Budgets aren't budging, and AI alone hasn't given care teams their time back, despite an avalanche of software options.

Compliance, documentation and measurement keep adding to the to-do list, whatever the care model. In a four-year study of 141 primary care physicians at one academic health system, they spent more than six hours in the electronic health record, including time outside clinic hours, for every eight hours of scheduled appointments, and that time kept rising from 2019 to 2023.²

If we want to genuinely evolve healthcare, we have to accept that humans need humans, and give care teams more time for the parts of care that give them energy. AI alone won't solve our problems. It has to fold into the realities of care. Agents can take on much of the remote work and box-ticking, and we should use them for it. But the problem isn't only paperwork. The opportunity is in the moments around the visit.

## The space between

There's a concept in architecture and design called liminal space: the space between two more important spaces. A corridor, a doorway, an entrance hall. Each exists to carry you into what comes next. The word comes from the Latin *limen*, threshold, and anthropologists have used it for more than a century to describe the in-between stage of a transition, when people have left one state and not yet arrived at the next.³

<Figure src={doorway} alt="Indigo line drawing of an open doorway in elevation, with its door swing drawn in plan below." caption="A doorway: built to carry you into what comes next." />

Immersive realities can be the liminal space clinics need before the visit. What if every clinic had one?

This is not about all of us being plugged in like the humans in WALL-E. If it ever heads that way, we should pull the plug.

What I'm talking about is enhancing the experience of care and making it a pleasant experience again, for patients and medical professionals at crucial points in the journey.

## What happens when patients are immersed

A patient settles into a chair in the clinic, puts on the headset, and an avatar asks what brought them in. When patients are immersed, they focus on the task at hand. The distractions fall away, and they can answer in their own words, so what they share informs the visit that follows.

<Figure src={avatar} wide alt="AugMend's avatar floating beside a still lake in a calm desert landscape at golden hour, with faint line drawings and dots over the scene." caption="Inside the headset: AugMend's avatar asks the questions, and the patient answers in their own words." />

That matters because so much goes unsaid today. In two national online surveys of 4,510 US adults, 61–81% said they had withheld medically relevant information from a clinician. The most common reason was not wanting to be judged or lectured.⁴

We want patients to feel unhurried and unjudged when they answer. One participant in their 60s, after the in-person VR session in our pilot with MIT, put the experience simply: "Being a first time experience, it was actually remarkably easy."⁵

An immersive world can also guide patients through the clinician-prescribed self-management exercises staff explain every day but rarely have time to walk through, like the underwater breathing exercise in the film. That attention goes back to another patient who needs it.

## What it asks of care teams

For medical professionals, it's a technology they can hand to a patient. It doesn't need them at the patient's side while it runs. Staff start a session in about 5 minutes. What follows is about 30 minutes of patient-reported history with no clinician in the room, while the team cares for other patients. Every finding in the report cites the patient's own words, and the clinician reviews and signs.

<Figure src={handover} alt="Hands passing a VR headset, overlaid with an indigo line tracing of the headset." caption="Staff start a session in about 5 minutes." />

When staff are short on time, it's not because the clinic is cutting back. It's because demand outpaces even the best triage. In those moments, we want patients to still get focused attention, in the clinic, before the visit.

## A new form factor, not a replacement

This isn't the solution to all of healthcare's problems. It's an extension of care, not a replacement for it.

But just as tablets changed how patients check in and share information, this is a new form factor. When six primary care practices handed patients a tablet at check-in to answer the screening questions nurses had been asking out loud, more than twice as many patients screened positive for depression, fall risk or intimate partner violence (19.5%, against 8.7% in the 60 days before). It was a before-and-after comparison in one health system, not a randomized trial. The authors suggest patients may find a tablet more private, and that staff may paraphrase questions when asking aloud.⁶ The lesson isn't that one device fixes disclosure. It's that how we ask shapes what we hear.

Our intake is a seated conversation in VR, in the clinic, before the visit, and the exercises are prescribed by the clinician.
